Vertical Fit Guide · Ada

Three verticals worth winning before the rest.

A reference for evaluating, pitching, and pricing Ada in three under-served local-service verticals. Built for a solo founder who needs vertical depth, not breadth.

Why these three

HVAC, plumbing, and electrical are crowded — six or more AI competitors already targeting them. The opportunity for a solo founder is not in those markets. It's in the verticals that share HVAC's economic shape (owner-operator, quote-driven, call-driven, follow-up-starved) but have nobody serious targeting them with AI yet.

Three verticals fit that profile and have additional structural advantages:

  • Bath/kitchen remodelers (1–5 person) — highest-ticket of the three. Single closed job pays for 18+ months of Ada.
  • Tree care / arborists — zero AI competition. Simple pitch. Faster sales cycle than remodelers.
  • Custom fencing / decking contractors — universal owner-operator profile. Ghost quotes are the daily pain.

This guide covers each: why it fits, the pitch, the numbers, where to find them, and how to test the market with five cheap conversations before committing.

The three-dimension fit framework

A vertical needs to score well on all three.

Dimension What to measure Why it matters
Ticket size × margin Average job revenue multiplied by typical gross margin Determines whether the owner can plausibly afford $400–1,500/mo for Ada
Sales-cycle gap Days between quote and decision Longer cycle = more follow-up value = bigger ROI on Ada's drafting
AI competition density Count of AI tools already targeting this vertical Crowded markets force feature/price competition. Underserved markets reward relationship + first-mover advantage.

The strategic insight

Vertical depth beats vertical breadth for a solo founder. Better to be the only AI for tree care than one of seven AI tools for HVAC. Three verticals is the right number — diversifies risk without spreading you thin.

How to pick which one to chase first

Personal network beats theoretical fit at this stage. The right answer for you depends on which of these you already have warm contacts in:

  • Know contractors or remodelers personally? Start with bath/kitchen remodelers. Highest ticket, best margins on Ada.
  • Know any tree-service owners? Start there. Simplest pitch, fastest close cycle.
  • Know fence/deck builders? Start there. Quote-follow-up pain is brutal and universal.
  • No warm contacts in any of them? Start with bath/kitchen remodelers. Even cold, the math is most compelling.

Realistic target

Three verticals × 5 customers each = 15 customers, ~$12K–18K MRR. Achievable for a solo founder within 6 months if you commit to one at a time, with overlap.

The highest-ROI vertical of the three. A single closed bath remodel covers two years of Ada. Almost no AI competition in this market because the industry-wide tools (Buildertrend, CoConstruct) are enterprise-tier and the small operators are completely unserved.

Why Ada fits this vertical

  • The economic argument is overwhelming. One closed $25K bath remodel that would have ghost-quoted = Ada paid for itself for 24+ months.

Validation playbook

How to test fit before committing

Don't invest 3 months building vertical-specific marketing for any of these without proof the pain resonates. Five conversations per vertical is enough to find out.

The five-conversation test

For each vertical you want to evaluate, schedule 5 conversations. Each is 20-30 minutes. Total investment: 2-3 hours per vertical. Decision quality improvement: enormous.

Where to source the 5

  1. Warm contacts first. Anyone in your personal network in the vertical = call them. The warmer the better.
  2. LinkedIn second-degree connections. Search "deck builder" or "arborist" in your city, filter for 2nd-degree, ask for intros.
  3. Local trade associations. NARI / TCIA / AFA chapter meetings. One conversation in person = 3 cold emails of value.
  4. Cold outreach only if all else fails. Use the vertical-specific cold email templates above. Expect 5-10% reply rate.

What to ask in each conversation

  1. Open with: "I'm researching how owners in [vertical] handle [specific pain — quote follow-up / missed calls / ghost quotes]. Would you mind sharing how it works at your company?"
  2. Then the discovery questions from that vertical's section.
  3. End with: "If I told you I built a tool that handled [specific role] for owners like you at $599-799/mo, what's your gut reaction?"

What "yes" sounds like

  • "I should be better about that." Universal pastor language, applies here. They know the gap exists.
  • "How would it know my voice?" They've already mentally bought it. Now they're asking implementation questions.
  • "What's the contract look like?" Closer-than-they-realize question.
  • "Can my partner see a demo?" Multi-person decision = real interest.
  • "What's the implementation timeline?" They're imagining themselves using it.

What "no" sounds like

  • "We follow up on every quote within 24 hours." Either true (they don't need it) or they're posturing. Either way, move on.
  • "$799 is too much." If they say that and don't ask "what's the ROI," they're not buyer-ready.
  • "I'd rather hire someone." Fair — but ask them what they've actually budgeted for hiring. Often nothing.
  • Silence after the price. Means it's outside their mental budget. Move on.

The decision rule after 5 conversations

Result Decision
4 or 5 said "yes" or "tell me more" Commit. Build vertical-specific cold email campaigns, landing pages, deck variants.
2 or 3 said yes Promising but not validated. Talk to 5 more before committing.
0 or 1 said yes Move on. The pain isn't there OR your framing is wrong. Try one of the other two verticals.

The bigger insight

5 conversations × 3 verticals = 15 hours of work. By the end you'll know with high confidence which of these three deserves the next 6 months of focus. Compare that to 6 months of building features for the wrong vertical.

When to add a fourth vertical

Don't add a fourth until you have:

  • At least 5 paying customers in your first vertical
  • Repeatable inbound or outbound process generating 2+ qualified conversations per week
  • A specific reason to think a fourth vertical fits (e.g., a customer referred someone, you found a personal contact, an unexpected lead came in)

Adding verticals before this is premature. Three is plenty. Three is enough to diversify risk while staying focused. Four is where solo founders start losing the thread.
